# Design Philosophy

The principles behind the design choices of Middle Semitic, each with its reason and the pages where it is carried out.

## Hebrew and Arabic on equal footing

Middle Semitic blends Hebrew and Arabic and gives the two relatively equal weight, even though they are very unequal in size.

- **The numbers.** Presented as a united front, Arabic has some 310 million speakers. The next two most common Semitic languages are southern (Ethiopian Semitic) and so do not figure into this Universal Language, which leaves Hebrew at about 6 million.
- **What the numbers hide.** Many Arabic "dialects" are mutually unintelligible, so the single figure for Arabic overstates its unity.
- **The region.** Given the volatile nature of the Middle East and North Africa, it is most prudent to put Arabic and Hebrew on relatively equal footing, and not to let the larger speaker count decide.
- **The script follows from it.** Choosing the alphabet (abjad) of either language would upset that balance. The existing common ground is Aramaic, so the script is Syriac, the classical written form of Aramaic. Many Middle Semitic words are identical to the Aramaic or Syriac form. See [[Orthography]].
- **The test of ease.** Middle Semitic must be easier for any one speaker to learn than any one other language is for that speaker. Arabic is hard for Hebrew speakers and Hebrew is hard for Arabic speakers; Middle Semitic is easier for each of them than the other's language.

How the languages are weighed word by word is described in [[Anthropology]].
