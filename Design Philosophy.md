# Design Philosophy

> Parent: [[Middle Semitic]] · Rationale for specific areas: [[Anthropology]] · [[Orthography]]

The principles behind the design choices of Middle Semitic, each with its reason and the pages where it is carried out.

## Hebrew and Arabic on equal footing

Middle Semitic blends Hebrew and Arabic and gives the two relatively equal weight, even though they are very unequal in size.

- **The numbers.** Presented as a united front, Arabic has some 310 million speakers. The next two most common Semitic languages are southern (Ethiopian Semitic) and so do not figure into this Universal Language, which leaves Hebrew at about 6 million.
- **What the numbers hide.** Many Arabic "dialects" are mutually unintelligible, so the single figure for Arabic overstates its unity.
- **The region.** Given the volatile nature of the Middle East and North Africa, it is most prudent to put Arabic and Hebrew on relatively equal footing, and not to let the larger speaker count decide.
- **The script follows from it.** Choosing the alphabet (abjad) of either language would upset that balance. The existing common ground is Aramaic, so the script is Syriac, the classical written form of Aramaic. Many Middle Semitic words are identical to the Aramaic or Syriac form. See [[Orthography]].
- **The test of ease.** Middle Semitic must be easier for any one speaker to learn than any one other language is for that speaker. Arabic is hard for Hebrew speakers and Hebrew is hard for Arabic speakers; Middle Semitic is easier for each of them than the other's language.

How the languages are weighed word by word is described in [[Anthropology]].

## Syriac as the script

Syriac is the script because it is the neutral common ground (see above). The reasons for the other choices, and what it costs the reader:

- **Why not Latin.** Latin is the script of the West and of colonial influence in the region, so adopting it would pick a side. Latin appears only as an aid: the Latin row on each lexicon entry is a transliteration for learners, never the script of the language.
- **Why an abjad.** A consonantal script shows the triconsonantal root, as the scripts of all the sources do, so the root stays visible in every derived word.
- **Why right-to-left.** Syriac is written RTL, so Hebrew and Arabic readers keep their direction of reading and their handwriting habits.
- **The learning cost.** Everyone learns the same small alphabet, so the cost is equal for Hebrew and Arabic speakers, and many letter shapes are already familiar from one or the other. This is part of the test of ease above.
- **Vowels.** True vowels are diacritics, as in Syriac. Like the sources' pointed and unpointed writing, they are optional: learners and unfamiliar words carry them, and experienced readers may drop them. The begadkefat dots likewise appear only in full spelling. See [[Orthography]].

## Choosing between Hebrew and Arabic: the common root first

When Hebrew and Arabic disagree on a word, neither simply wins. Every word is researched: the source languages are polled and Proto-Semitic is consulted. This is why Middle Semitic needs a constant human touch and a lot of research. Proto-Semitic comes first because an appeal to the most ancient is an authority above the modern warring factions, without picking a favorite among today's parties. The decision runs in two stages.

1. **Appeal to the broadest root.** If a root or word exists across the region and in Proto-Semitic, Middle Semitic uses it, even where some source languages have since moved on from it. The example is *louse* ([[Swadesh]] № 48): the root Q-M-L exists across the region and in Proto-Semitic, but Hebrew and the rest of Northwest Semitic have moved on to a different root (Hebrew כנה *kinnâ*). Middle Semitic backs up and takes the common root, even though it takes digging deep into Rabbinic literature to find the Hebrew trace of it, קַלְמָנָה.
2. **Fall back to the modern majority.** Only when the search for a broad word or root across the region is exhausted does Middle Semitic switch to a modern majority strategy, choosing what most of the modern source languages use. The majority is counted by number of source languages, not by number of speakers. Hebrew is given a little extra weight, which keeps Arabic's many dialects from simply outvoting it; even so, this has produced several choices that lean on the Arabic dialects, such as [[Lexicon/ܪܣܘܠܐ|ܪܣܘܠܐ]] *rasûlâ* "messenger" (in place of Hebrew שלח) and [[Lexicon/ܟܡܢ|ܟܡܢ]] *kaman* "also" (from the colloquial Arabic *kamān*).

See also the treatment of the proto-language in [[Anthropology]].

## An equal vote for three faiths

Jews, Muslims and Christians get an equal vote, and the most delicate place that shows is religious vocabulary. There is no absolutely neutral ground, so the work is finding the most neutral ground available, which is slow and painstaking.

- **Names are not common nouns.** *Allah* is a proper name. The common noun *god* is a different word, [[Lexicon/ܐܝܠܗܐ|ܐܝܠܗܐ]] *êlhâ*, which is a little bit of each of the sources and so makes a good neutral choice.
- **The important texts of each religion are worked through.** [[Dialogue]] carries the Shahada and the Jesus Prayer; [[Torah]] carries Genesis 1:1 and the Shema. Each is a test of whether the vocabulary holds up in the text it has to serve.
- **The hardest case is Jesus's name.** The non-Christian languages have two names for him: the polemical יֵשׁוּ and عيسى, and the historic or Christian יֵשׁוּעַ and يسوع. Middle Semitic blends all of them into ܝܣܘܥ.

## Sounds and syllables

The guiding meta-principle is the same as everywhere: Middle Semitic must be easier to learn than anyone else's language. For sounds, that means it is the easiest soundscape for each source community, though not necessarily rock-bottom easy.

**Consonants.**

- Everyone has the voiced and unvoiced stops, but the fricatives are sketchy. The common ground is the *begadkefat* principle: neither pronunciation is forced on anyone. ܦ can be /p/ or /f/, and the two are the same letter. See [[Phonology]] and [[Orthography]].
- Some effort remains. Hebrew speakers must think of ʁ as ʿ, though they have the sound. They must also learn an alveolar r, but they would have to learn that for anyone else's language too.

**Syllables.** Proto-Semitic had only CV and CVC syllables, so that is the baseline, which may be expanded later. What each tradition forbids differs, and each kind of cluster is forbidden by someone, except in the middle of a word:

- Classical and Standard Arabic allow no initial clusters at all: *ktub* becomes اُكْتُبْ.
- Hebrew allowed initial clusters, but it varied by dialect: *sfarim* may or may not have an epenthetic schwa, depending on the speaker.
- Everyone allows medial clusters across a syllable or word boundary, as in מִכְתָּב.

| Environment | Standard / Classical Arabic | Modern / Biblical Hebrew | Aramaic / Syriac | Proto-Semitic |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Word-initial (#CC-) | Forbidden (inserts *ʾu-* / *ʾi-*) | Allowed (*ktav*, *zman*) | Pervasive (*ktābā*) | Forbidden |
| Word-medial (-CC-) | Allowed (VC.CV) | Allowed (VC.CV) | Allowed (VC.CV) | Allowed |
| Word-medial (-CCC-) | Forbidden | Forbidden | Restricted | Forbidden |
| Word-final (-CC#) | Pausa only (*bint*, *kalb*) | Avoided (segolation: *melech*) | Allowed, or resolved by a suffix | Restricted |

Middle Semitic's syllable inventory of CV and CVC, with no onset or coda clusters, is the part every source accepts, and a cluster arises only across a syllable boundary.

## Archaisms and an Old Middle Semitic

Some features of Middle Semitic are deliberately archaic: the frozen construct state, VSO as the "archaic" word order, the frozen dual. The reason is that every one of the source languages has a very ancient prestige form:

- the Arabics are supposedly united in their study of Classical Arabic, in theory;
- Aramaic has Classical Syriac;
- Israeli has Biblical Hebrew.

The first millennia BC and AD are where much, much more unity can be found than today. The goal of a Universal Language is to promote unity and communication, and every speaker of the source languages can get better at Middle Semitic by studying the history of their own language.

This leads naturally to the idea of an **Old Middle Semitic**, a contrivance but a convenience: the biggest part of the overlap in the Venn diagram of the source languages comes from the past, so Middle Semitic needs a past of its own. A functioning Old Middle Semitic will not be developed as a separate language. It already exists in enough form in the archaic features above: VSO, the dual and the construct state. See [[Morphophonology]] and [[Morphosyntax]].

## Word order and prepositions

- **SVO is the strict default.** Three reasons point the same way: Middle Semitic has no case marking, so a fixed order has to carry who did what to whom and SVO is the clearest; SVO is the modern majority among the source languages; and it is the order that is easiest for each community (Hebrew speakers use it daily, and Arabic speakers use it in the dialects and as an option in Standard Arabic). VSO survives as the archaic order (see the section above). See [[Morphosyntax]].
- **Eight small prepositions conjugate; the rest are nouns.** Hebrew, Arabic and Aramaic all inflect a core set of short prepositions with pronominal suffixes, so that set is the shared core. Every other "preposition" is a noun in the construct state, which reuses a pattern learners already know, keeps the system regular and keeps the conjugating set small. The construct state is also the frozen archaic feature, so this ties prepositions to the Old Middle Semitic idea. See [[Prepositions]].

## Regular, guessable forms

A Universal Language is an international auxiliary language, so a certain amount of templatic layout is unavoidable, and good. The form of a word needs to be guessable from a regular pattern, like a correlative chart.

- **Esperanto is the cautionary tale, but for its vocabulary.** It receives a failing grade mostly because its vocabulary is a scattershot of Western Europe with sprinkles of Eastern Europe. Its grammar is too regular, but only because none of its source languages is anywhere near as agglutinative as it is.
- **Middle Semitic is triconsonantal like its sources, but more regular and predictable than any of them,** which are all fully natural languages. That regularity is part of the design principle of a Universal Language.
- **The rule of thumb is fidelity unless confusing.** A source irregularity is kept by default. It is regularized only when it breaks a learnable pattern, when the sources split on it (so the regular form is the neutral ground), or when it would need a per-word lookup with no payoff in familiarity for any source.
- **There are limits to regularity.** One is the "third" gender, the crypto-feminine: nouns that are feminine in agreement but do not end in the feminine *-t*. Taking regularity to the Esperanto level, too far, would have meant making those words masculine or eliminating gender altogether.
- **Another quasi-naturalistic feature is the chiastic gender agreement of the numbers 3 to 10,** which all of the source languages share.

## Stress

Stress is predictable, and it is a compromise by design, chosen both to be easy to learn and to give every source community something familiar. The rule takes in weight (Arabic), the ultimate syllable (Hebrew) and the penultimate syllable (Aramaic): a closed final syllable takes the stress, otherwise the penult. See [[Phonology]].

- **Exceptions.** The rule has exceptions, mainly to do with the clitic prepositions. They have not all been listed yet. They also help keep the language approachable and similar to the source languages.
- **Different texts feel different.** The Shahada, in [[Dialogue]], is a place where Middle Semitic starts to feel like Arabic stress, while the Shema, in [[Torah]], is Hebrew-adjacent.
- **The goal** is to keep the language feeling as familiar as possible, so that everyone experiences it as "close to home", while still following regular rules, as in the principle above.

## International vocabulary

There is such a thing as international vocabulary. It is sometimes English-flavoured, but that can largely be avoided. Using it is preferable to picking one source language over the others.

- **A new root.** A modern word can be given a new triconsonantal root. "Wifi", for example, could come from a new root W-Y-P.
- **Coffee as the model.** [[Lexicon/ܩܗܘܐ|ܩܗܘܐ]] (coffee) comes from Q-H-W. The core idea can be taken to be "caffeine" or "stimulate", and verbs, adjectives and more nouns can be derived from that root.
- **The goal is to borrow so hard that the word becomes native,** minimizing the presence of foreign words while still appealing outside the disputes between the source languages that cannot be resolved.

## A language is its words plus its grammar

A language **is** its words plus its grammar, and this vault documents all of both. With a newly invented language there is nothing extant to appeal to, so all of it has to be created from scratch. There is no corpus of texts in Middle Semitic; the Peshitta and other Syriac documents come closest, but they are in another language.

- **A finished entry.** A lexical entry needs all of its etymology documented, its derivational morphology, all the possible inflections of the word, cross-language comparisons, and it needs to be woven into the fabric of Middle Semitic.
- **The Lexipedia.** The next phase is the Lexipedia project, taken from the book of the same name by Mark Rosenberg. It divides the vocabulary into dozens of semantic domains, so that there are words for all basic needs and so that subtle differences among the words are distinguished. This phase is marked by words having their own life in the language, and not just existing as glosses of terms in the source languages.
- **Keeping entries current.** The `date-last-perfect` property records when everything on an entry's page was last brought up to date. As the standards develop and improve, it is the sign to update the page and document the new requirements.
