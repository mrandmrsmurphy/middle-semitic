# Phonology

> Parent: [[Middle Semitic]] · Areal survey: [[Anthropology#Phonology]]

Middle Semitic sounds consist of 22 consonants and 5 vowels.

## Consonants

The basic shape of MS is the shared liquids (m, n, l, r, w, y), the begadkefat spirants (b/v, g/ɣ, d/ð, k/x, p/f, t/θ), other fricatives (h, ʕ, ħ, z, s, š), and the “emphatics” (ṣ, ṭ, q, ʔ). Notice that Semitic studies has a long history of romanization which does not precisely map onto IPA.

## Vowels

The vowels are the basic five common throughout most of the world, without diphthongs. A sixth vowel, schwa (/ə/), is an exceedingly marginal phoneme: it occurs in the fast speech of clitics (the main prepositions) and is explicitly part of the grammar only in the imperative, where it fills the first syllable of a stem that begins with a consonant cluster. Imperatives are written with a vowel in the first syllable and are stressed on the next syllable.

## Phonotactics

Because of the triconsonantal/infixing nature of noun declension and verbal conjugation, it is necessary to thoroughly understand Middle Semitic phonotactics in order to speak the language. Pharyngeal/uvular and glottal consonants are labeled “guttural”, i.e.

The only syllable shapes are CV and CVC. There are no onset clusters and no coda clusters, so a vowel-initial word begins with a glottal stop. Geminate consonants are not clusters.

- ḥ, h, ʿ, and ʾ

The glottal stop, interestingly, overlaps with the next category, known as *mater lectionis*. These “mothers of reading” began as an aid to help guess the vowels of the consonantal text. For us, ʾ is the hint to an /a/, /j/ is the hint of an /i/, and /w/ is the hint of an /u/. (Of course, they have consonantal uses too.) A mater *y* may indicate an /i/ or an /e/. A mater *w* may indicate an /o/ or a /u/.

- ʾ, y, and w

Almost all “weakness” of stems comes down to what happens when one of these letters is in a medial and/or final coda.

- Coda gutturals (but not aleph) disappear in pronunciation, though they are always written. There may be some effect on the preceding vowel, but maybe not even that.
- Coda maters force a change in the vowel before to conform.
- Coda *n* is written, but is actually gemination of the following consonant.

## Stress

Stress is predictable and falls on one syllable of the word, counted over the whole word including any suffixes (a proclitic such as *pi-*, *bi-* or *wa-* is not counted):

- If the **final syllable is closed** (CVC), it takes the stress: *ma.ˈlak*, *ba.ˈraq*, *ma.la.ˈkat*, *mal.ˈkîn*.
- Otherwise the **penultimate syllable** takes the stress: *ˈmal.kâ*, *ˈba.râ*, *ba.ˈhî.mâ*, *ma.ˈlak.nî*.
- A one-syllable word carries its own stress.
- **Schwa** (the imperative and clitic vowel) is never stressed: the stress moves to the next syllable (*mə.ˈlak!*, *mə.ˈlak.nâ!*, *bə.ˈnê!*).

There are two exceptions to the rule, and both keep the stress off a syllable that the rule would otherwise choose:

- **The imperative.** The first syllable of an imperative holds the schwa (*mə.lak!*) and can never be stressed. When the rule would stress it, which happens in a two-syllable imperative with an open final syllable, the stress moves to the next syllable: *bə.ˈnê!* (from *banê*), not *ˈbə.nê!*. Where the stress already falls elsewhere, nothing changes (*mə.ˈlak!*, *mə.ˈlak.nâ!*).
- **The prefixing prepositions.** The clitic prepositions *li- bi- pi- ki- mi- ʿa-* attach to the following word and never take the stress on their own syllable, whether they are said in full (*bi-*) or with a schwa in fast speech (*bə-*). They are not counted in finding the stress of the word they join (*pi-ˈbêt*, *li-ˈmal.kâ*), so the stress is always that of the host word. The free prepositions (*min*, *ʿal-*, *ʿim*, *bên*) are words with stress of their own.

The rule is a compromise among the sources: Aramaic puts stress on the penult, Hebrew on the last syllable, and Arabic follows syllable weight.

In the romanization of a lexical entry, syllables are separated by dots and the stressed syllable is preceded by *ˈ* (a modifier letter, distinct from the apostrophe-like *ʾ* and *ʿ*). Words of one syllable are left unmarked. Paradigm cells are not marked.
