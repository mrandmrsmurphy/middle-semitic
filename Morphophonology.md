# Morphophonology

> Parent: [[Middle Semitic]] · Syntax: [[Morphosyntax]]

*Rationale: the forms are meant to be guessable from a regular template, more regular than any source language, since a Universal Language needs that. Where the sources share an irregularity it is kept (the crypto-feminine nouns, the chiastic agreement of the numerals 3 to 10) rather than flattened Esperanto-style. The frozen construct state and frozen dual are deliberate archaisms: every source has an ancient prestige form, and the first millennia BC and AD are where the languages overlap most. See [[Design Philosophy#Regular, guessable forms]] and [[Design Philosophy#Archaisms and an Old Middle Semitic]].*

- Three numbers: singular and plural, with duals only for body parts, glasses, pants. The dual is frozen, and not productive.
- SVO word order, though others are possible with clefting or in questions.
- No case marking
- Two genders in agreement, three noun declensions (see [[#Nouns]]); numerals 3–10 agree chiastically (see [[#Numerals]])
- Two states — regular and construct. Construct is frozen, not productive. The genitive clitic (cp. English *of*) is *dal* — [[Lexicon/ܕܠ|ܕܠ]]
- Definiteness can only be achieved through proper nouns or the definite article [[Lexicon/ܗܠ|ܗܠ]] (*hal*). No indefinite article.
- [[Lexicon/ܗ|ܗ]] (*ha*) is the sentence-initial polar question particle
- No pausal forms
- Adjectives
  - Agree in number, gender, (and state) with their head
  - Elatives are uninflected
  - No agreement for copulative clause
- Only one, uninflecting relative pronoun [[Lexicon/ܠܝܕ|ܠܝܕ]] (*lîd*)

Notice that we do not maintain the gender distinction in the second person plural.

## Tri-consonantal

The heart of Semitic conjugation and declension is the tri-consonantal root, or non-concatenative morphology. Most words are imagined to come from a particular set of three consonants, with vowels and other consonants inserted in and around them in order to form all parts of speech and such. The catch is that certain letters cause problems:

### The semi-vowels

Because three letters are used to hint at or spell out vowels, they blur the lines and alter conjugation tables. /ʔ/, /y/, and /w/ (aleph, yod and waw) tend to collapse syllables into vowels. However, they can be in a coda and can geminate. Strange things happen when we consider these semi-vowels butting up against real vowels, especially since MS does not have diphthongs.

- a+y = ê
- a+' = â
- a+w = ô
- i+' = ê
- i+y = î
- i+w = î
- u+y = û

### The gutturals

Three consonants happen so far back in the throat that they take too long to prepare to say. /ʕ/, /ḥ/, /h/ (ayin, ḥet and he) cannot be geminated, but they can be in the coda. They are always written, in every form of the root; a guttural in the coda is written but not pronounced (see [[Phonology]]). Because they cannot be geminated, a guttural as the middle radical of the D, Dp and tD stems is compensated by lengthening the vowel after it, written with an aleph (the vowel before it is already long, *î* or *û*): D *rîʿâ-*, not *rîʿʿa-*; in a III-yod root the final *y* then contracts, giving *rîʿê* (ܪܝܥܐܝ). Model page: [[Lexicon/ܪܥܝ]].

### Nun

In ancient times, nun could not be in the coda, so it caused gemination of the following consonant. This is no longer the case, but it does mean certain forms can be surprising.

## Nouns

Nouns come in three declensions:

1. masculine
2. true feminine (f² below)
3. feminine-appearing / masculine-in-the-singular (f¹ below)

The third declension is a "third gender" in all but name. Agreement has only two genders (adjectives, verbs and numerals see masculine or feminine), but the crypto-feminine nouns are a class of their own: they *agree* as feminine yet have no feminine ending in the singular (*ʿenâ*, not *ʿenatâ*), and their plural and construct are the feminine ones. A crypto-feminine noun therefore takes feminine adjectives, verbs and numerals while looking masculine in the singular. The class is decided by the gender of the Hebrew, Arabic and Aramaic cognates (majority), when the noun has no *-t* of its own; the dual, where there is one, is for body parts. Examples: *ʿenâ* "eye", *ʾarṣâ* "earth", *katpâ* "shoulder".

**Masculine** — *day* ܝܘܡܐ, which still occurs in the dual.

|             | singular    | dual          | plural        |
| ----------- | ----------- | ------------- | ------------- |
| regular     | *yômâ* ܝܘܡܐ | *yômîm* ܝܘܡܝܡ | *yômîn* ܝܘܡܝܢ |
| *construct* | *yôm* ܝܘܡ   | *yômî* ܝܘܡܝ   | *yômî* ܝܘܡܝ   |

**Crypto-feminine** (f¹) — *eye/spring* ܥܝܢܐ. Feminine in gender but with masculine endings: the singular has no feminine ending, and the plural and construct are feminine. The dual is for body parts.

| | singular | dual | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| regular | *ʿenâ* ܥܝܢܐ | *ʿenîm* ܥܝܢܝܡ | *ʿenût* ܥܝܢܘܬ |
| *construct* | *ʿenat* ܥܝܢܬ | *ʿenû* ܥܝܢܘ | *ʿenû* ܥܝܢܘ |

**True feminine** (f²) — *queen* ܡܠܟܬܐ, which has no dual because it is not a body part.

| | singular | plural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| regular | *malkatâ* ܡܠܟܬܐ | *malkût* ܡܠܟܘܬ |
| *construct* | *malkat* ܡܠܟܬ | *malkû* ܡܠܟܘ |

A true-feminine body part has a dual too, built on the feminine stem with *-îm*, like the Hebrew *-atayim* and Arabic *-atān*; its construct is *-atû*, distinct from the plural construct. *Palm* ܪܚܬܐ:

| | singular | dual | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| regular | *raḥtâ* ܪܚܬܐ | *raḥatîm* ܪܚܬܝܡ | *raḥût* ܪܚܘܬ |
| *construct* | *raḥat* ܪܚܬ | *raḥatû* ܪܚܬܘ | *raḥû* ܪܚܘ |

The dual is frozen: only body parts, glasses and pants have one, apart from a few frozen words such as *day*. Other nouns use the plural for two. The vowel of the stem varies from word to word (*malkâ*, *ʿenâ*, *ʾarṣâ*), and may be **u**, **a** or **i** (or **e** or **o** in foreign-based words, or by contraction: *yawm* contracts to *yôm*). For example, *king* is **malkâ, malkîn** (construct **malak, malkî**), *earth* is **ʾarṣâ, ʾarṣût** (construct **ʾarṣat, ʾarṣû**).

Nouns from **III-yod roots** (*gadê* 'kid') end in long *ê* in the singular, because the root's final *y* contracts with the ending. Before the plural ending it shows as *î*: **gadê, gadîn** (construct **gadi**, **gadî**). In the plain spelling the yod is written once.

### Pronouns

The pronouns may seem strange for Indo-Europeans, but are utterly normal for Semitic speakers. There are independent forms which can only serve as the subject of a clause. Another set of forms serves three functions: they attach to verbs to mark direct objects, to nouns to indicate genitival possession, and to the small prepositions (*ʿalnî* 'upon me' — see [[Prepositions]] and [[Morphosyntax]] for the paradigms). ## Numerals

The numbers 3 to 10 show **chiastic** (reverse) agreement, a feature all of the source languages share: the numeral takes the opposite gender from the noun it counts. The counted noun is plural.

| counting | feminine noun | masculine noun |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *šalaš* ܫܠܫ | *šalašâ* ܫܠܫܐ | *šalašatâ* ܫܠܫܬܐ |

So the lexical form (*-â*, like a masculine adjective) goes with a feminine noun, and a masculine noun takes the form in *-atâ*, built like a feminine singular noun. The crypto-feminine nouns count as feminine: *šalašâ ʿenût* "three eyes" (the lexical form), but *šalašatâ yômîn* "three days" (the *-atâ* form). A true feminine takes the lexical form too: *šalašâ malkût* "three queens".

| | counting | feminine noun | masculine noun |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | *šalaš* | *šalašâ* | *šalašatâ* |
| 4 | *arbaʿ* | *arbaʿâ* | *arbaʿatâ* |
| 5 | *ḥamsâ* | *ḥamsâ* | *ḥamsatâ* |
| 6 | *sêš* | *sêšâ* | *sêšatâ* |
| 7 | *sabʿâ* | *sabʿâ* | *sabʿatâ* |
| 8 | *šamnâ* | *šamnâ* | *šamnatâ* |
| 9 | *têsʿâ* | *têsʿâ* | *têsʿatâ* |
| 10 | *ʿašrâ* | *ʿašrâ* | *ʿašratâ* |

The bare counting form (arithmetic, counting on the fingers) drops the final *-â* unless the bare form would end in an illegal cluster, which is why *ḥamsâ* and *sabʿâ* keep it. The other numbers do not reverse: *one* agrees with its noun (m *waḥad*, f *waḥdâ*), *two* is a dual with a single form for both genders (*šanyîm*), and above ten the counted noun is singular. See [[Morphosyntax]] for the syntax.

## Verbs

A verb has up to seven **stems**, named G, Gp, D, Dp, C, Cp and tD. G is the basic stem; D intensifies or specializes it; C is causative; tD is reflexive or reciprocal; the "p" stems are the passives. Each lexical entry says which stems it has: **no stem is assumed**, and a verb may lack any of them (*malak* has no D, Dp or tD). The entry's Stems table gives each stem's meaning, which is lexical, not predictable from the template. Every stem has a past, a non-past, a participle and an infinitive, and the active stems have an imperative; the tables below show the strong root *C₁C₂C₃* with the model verb ܟܬܒ.

| Stem | Past 3m | Non-past 3m | Participle | Infinitive | Template |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| G | *katab* | *yaktab* | *maktab-* | *laktab* | C₁aC₂aC₃ |
| Gp | *kûtab* | *yûktab* | *maktûb-* | *laktûb* | û after the first consonant of the form |
| D | *kîttab* | *yakîttab* | *makîttab-* | *lakîttab* | C₁îC₂C₂aC₃ |
| Dp | *kûttab* | *yûkîttab* | *makûttab-* | *lakûttab* | C₁ûC₂C₂aC₃ |
| C | *šaktab* | *yašaktab* | *mašaktab-* | *lašaktab* | š- + C₁C₂aC₃ |

The imperative is the second-person non-past without its prefix, so it exists for G, D, C and tD only. - Passive part. *maMLûK-* ܡܡܠܘܟ
- Passive inf. *laMLûK* ܠܡܠܘܟ
- The passive changes the vowel after the first consonant of the form to long *û*, written with a waw, so it shows in unvocalized text. The first consonant is the prefix in the non-past (*yamlak* → *yûmlak*) and the first radical in the past (*malak* → *mûlak*), and the same holds for every stem. The participle and infinitive have no vowel there (*ma-* and *la-* are prefixes), so the second vowel changes instead. The imperative has no passive. All other endings follow the active.

**The derived stems.** Each is built on the G stem and keeps the same endings and prefixes, so only the stem changes. The model for all seven is [[Lexicon/ܟܬܒ]] (G write, D inscribe, C dictate, tD correspond).

- **D** puts the long vowel *î* after the first radical and doubles the second: *kîttab*, non-past *yakîttab*, participle *makîttab-*, imperative *kîttab!*. The *î* shows in the unvocalized spelling (ܟܝܬܒ), because the gemination mark is optional and is left out; this is the same job as the *i* of Hebrew *piʿel*.
- **Dp** is the passive of D. In the past *û* takes the place of the *î* (*kûttab*); in the non-past the prefix vowel becomes *û* and the stem keeps its *î* (*yûkîttab*). The participle is *makûttab-*.
- **C** (causative) prefixes *š-* to the root, as Akkadian does with its Š stem: *šaktab*, non-past *yašaktab*, imperative *šaktab!*. No other *š-* prefix exists in the language, so the stem is recognizable at a glance.
- **Cp** is the passive of C: *û* follows the first consonant of the form, which is the *š-* in the past (*šûktab*) and the prefix in the non-past (*yûšaktab*, where *û* replaces the prefix vowel and the *š-* stays on the stem). The participle is *mašûktab-*.
- **tD** is *ti-* (written with just ܬ, a short *i*, distinct from the *ta-* of the non-past) plus the D stem: *tikîttab*, non-past *yatikîttab*, imperative *tikîttab!*. It is reflexive or reciprocal, and where reciprocal it assumes a plural subject. Naming it tD (not T) lets Arabic speakers read it as stem V.

Gp and Dp are spelled alike in the unvocalized script unless the *shadda* is written (ܟܘܬܒ for both *kûtab* and *kûttab*), as in Hebrew and Arabic; the same overlap exists elsewhere in the system and is accepted. Stative verbs have no passives.

**Derived stems of weak and guttural roots.** The contractions given for G apply in every stem (*aw* → *ô*, *ay* → *ê*, *aʾ* → *â*), and the first vowel wins when two meet. Gutturals (ʿ ḥ h) are always written; a middle guttural cannot double, so in D, Dp and tD the following vowel is lengthened instead (see [[#The gutturals]]). A middle aleph, by contrast, is a full consonant and doubles: D *sîʾʾal*, Dp *sûʾʾal*, tD *tisîʾʾal* (model: [[Lexicon/ܣܐܠ]]), and in a III-yod root as well (D *rîʾʾê*, [[Lexicon/ܪܐܝ]]). A III-yod or III-waw root has the same endings in every stem as in G (*banê*, C *šabnê*, [[Lexicon/ܒܢܝ]]). In a hollow root the middle *w* or *y* is a consonant in the derived stems, so it doubles in D (*bîyyat*) and the root is otherwise regular.

**Statives.** A stative verb ("he was good") has the vowel *î* as the second vowel of its stem: past *ḥasîn*, non-past *yaḥsîn*, imperative *ḥəsîn!*, participle *maḥsîn-*, infinitive *laḥsîn*. The *î* shows in every form, so a stative is identifiable at a glance, and it has no passive. The adjective on the same stem adds *-â* (*ṣaʿîr* "he was small", *ṣaʿîrâ* "small"). The test for a stative is transitivity, not the English gloss. Because the *î* separates the second and third radicals, a geminate root is regular (*barîr*), and a III-waw root keeps its *w* as a consonant after the *î*, as in *banyû*. Its derived stems are those of a strong verb, with the meanings of making or causing the state (D *qîddas* "sanctify", C *šaqdas* "consecrate", tD "be hallowed"). Model pages: [[Lexicon/ܚܣܝܢ]] (G stem), [[Lexicon/ܩܕܝܣ]] (all stems).

**Geminate roots are not weak.** A root whose last two radicals are the same (B-R-R) is conjugated as a strong root in every stem: nothing contracts or simplifies, and the repeated radical is written wherever the template calls for it (G *barîr*; D *bîrrar*, Dp *bûrrar*, C *šabrar*, Cp *šûbrar*, tD *tibîrrar*). Model page: [[Lexicon/ܒܪܝܪ]].
**Hollow verbs.** In a hollow root (II-waw or II-yod) the central vowel is part of the root and is always present, as a contraction (*aw* → *ô*, *ay* → *ê*) or a pure mater (*û*); which one is lexical. The CVC stem therefore persists in every form and the verb just takes prefixes and suffixes (*qôm*, *qômat*, *yaqôm*). Hollow verbs have no marked stative. A hollow verb that has a passive forms it by the general rule, with û after the first consonant of the form: *dôs* → *dûs* in the past, *yadôs* → *yûdôs* in the non-past (model: [[Lexicon/ܕܘܣ]], which has G and Gp). Verbs that have no passive cannot be told from a passive by form. In the derived stems the middle radical is a full consonant and takes its place in the template (D *bîyyat*, C *šabyat*, tD *tibîyyat*); the full seven-stem models are [[Lexicon/ܒܝܬ]] (II-yod) and [[Lexicon/ܩܘܡ]] (II-waw). In a II-waw root the C and Cp stems keep the *w* (*šaqwam*, *šûqwam*), as the template has no *aw* there. Basic model page: [[Lexicon/ܩܘܡ]].

**Weak third radicals.** The third radical (ʾ, w, y) is always written. It contracts with the preceding *a* (*ay* → *ê*, *aw* → *ô*, *aʾ* → *â*), and if a vowel then follows, the first vowel wins: the template *banay* + *at* gives *banêt*, not *banayat*. Where no *a* precedes it the radical is a consonant (*banyû*). The feminine singular imperative and the 2f non-past therefore sound like the masculine (*tabnê*), and the plural non-past loses its vowel (*yabnên*); the spelling keeps every letter. Model page: [[Lexicon/ܒܢܝ]].

**Weak first radicals.** The three maters (ʾ, y, w) in first position fuse with the vowel of the prefix: aleph is quiescent and lengthens it (*ʾakal* → *âkal*, *yaʾkal* → *yâkal*), while *aw* contracts to *ô* and *ay* to *ê* (*yawbal* → *yôbal*, *yaynaq* → *yênaq*). In the passive non-past the mater reverts to a consonant after the *û* (aleph as a glottal stop), which keeps the passive distinct from the active: *yûʾkal*, *yûwlad*, *yûynaq*; the passive past is *ʾûkal*, *wûlad*, *yûnaq*. In the C stem the *aw* of an I-waw root contracts too (*šawlad* → *šôlad*, non-past *yašôlad*, Cp non-past *yûšôlad*), while the Cp past keeps its *w* after the *û* (*šûwlad*); see [[Lexicon/ܘܠܕ]]. The I-yod C stem contracts likewise (*šaynaq* → *šênaq*, non-past *yašênaq*), and the Cp past keeps its *y* (*šûynaq*); see [[Lexicon/ܝܢܩ]]. Model pages: [[Lexicon/ܐܟܠ]] (I-aleph), [[Lexicon/ܘܒܠ]] (I-waw), [[Lexicon/ܝܢܩ]] (I-yod). When the prefix aleph of the 1c non-past meets a root-initial aleph, the root aleph eats it, so there is a single aleph (*ʾâkal* ܐܟܠ), unlike the passive (*ʾûʾkal* ܐܘܐܟܠ), where the *û* keeps the two apart. Weak verbs have many collapsed or identical forms; that is expected.
